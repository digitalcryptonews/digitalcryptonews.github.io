Bridge Relayers Earn the Gap for Faster Fills

How do bridge relayers make money? They front destination funds, recover the deposit later, and keep a fee that prices gas, locked capital and finality risk.

The Finality DeskNov 28, 2026 · 05:38 UTC5 min read#8cb3bc

Bridge relayers make money by paying out on the destination chain before they receive repayment, then keeping a fee from the transfer. When the source deposit is still awaiting finality, the relayer also takes on the risk that it could be reversed.

  • The relayer pays the recipient from its own destination-chain funds.
  • After proving the fill, it seeks repayment from the protocol’s pools.
  • The fee covers destination gas, capital tied up until repayment, and a margin for risk.

Take an illustrative transfer: you deposit 1,000 USDC on one chain and receive 998 USDC on another. The relayer later gets back about 1,000 USDC from the protocol’s pools. The two-USDC gap is its gross fee; destination gas and other costs reduce what it keeps as profit.

How does a relayer earn money from a bridge transfer?

A relayer earns the difference between the value it pays out and the value it later receives, after accounting for its costs. In the illustrative 1,000-to-998 transfer, you get funds sooner, while the relayer waits to claim repayment. Its fee is the price of that advance, not a simple markup detached from the transfer’s mechanics.

On Across, relayers fill transfers with their own funds and are repaid from the protocol’s pools. They choose which chain to be repaid on. That choice matters because repayment timing and the cost of moving or holding funds affect how long capital stays committed.

The fee has three main components. Destination gas pays for the transaction that delivers funds. The capital cost reflects funds unavailable for another fill while repayment is pending. The margin compensates for the risks of completing the transfer and for the relayer’s operating costs. The exact quote can vary with the transfer and the conditions on both chains.

Relayers compete to fill transfers, which can push fees toward the cost of providing the service. An aggregator such as lifi bridge compares available route quotes, so a practical check is to compare the amount you will receive and the estimated time across routes. For what a lifi bridge relayer fee covers, compare the quote’s fee with the gas and capital costs behind the fill.

After the payout, the relayer proves that it filled the transfer and seeks repayment. That step connects the early destination payment to the later pool repayment: it is why the relayer can pay you first without treating the source deposit as immediately available cash. When repayment is slow, more capital remains tied up, so the same relayer may have less capacity for other fills and earn less on its funds over time. Gas spikes also cut into the fee left after costs.

Why does source-chain finality affect relayer fees?

Source-chain finality affects fees because a relayer that pays before the deposit is final risks paying out for a deposit that later disappears. A chain reorganization can reverse a source transaction after the relayer has already delivered funds on the destination chain. The relayer may then have paid the recipient without a valid deposit to support repayment.

That risk changes the speed-versus-cost trade-off. Filling sooner gives the user a faster transfer, but exposes the relayer to a greater chance that the source deposit will be reversed. On chains with slower finality, relayers may wait longer before filling or charge more to price in the risk of filling early. A higher fee in that case reflects a specific failure mode: the source payment could be erased after the destination payout.

For you, the fee and estimated time are linked. A fast quote can mean the relayer is accepting more finality risk; a slower fill can give the source deposit more time to become final. Read both together when choosing a route, rather than judging the transfer by its fee alone.

Can anyone become a bridge relayer?

Whether anyone can relay depends on the bridge’s participation rules and the capital and operational capacity the work requires. A relayer needs funds available on destination chains and must be able to manage fills, costs, repayment, and the risk of a source reorganization. The Across model described here uses relayers that supply their own funds for fills.

What happens if no relayer fills my transfer?

If no relayer fills a transfer, the destination payout has not happened yet, so you have not received the bridged funds. Availability can depend on whether a relayer finds the transfer worth filling at the quoted price and under current network conditions. Check the transfer’s status and available route information before deciding what to do next.

Are relayers and solvers the same thing?

Relayer and solver can describe overlapping roles, but the terms depend on the bridge or protocol. Here, a relayer is the party that fronts destination-chain funds and later claims repayment. A solver is a broader term used in some cross-chain systems for a participant that executes or fulfills a requested route; check the protocol’s terminology to know which duties it includes.

Related questions

How much capital does a relayer need?

A relayer needs enough working capital to cover the fills it chooses to make while earlier transfers await repayment. The amount depends on the size and frequency of those fills, how quickly repayment arrives, and which chains it serves.

Why do fees rise for small transfers?

Destination gas can take up a larger share of a small transfer, leaving less of the fee as profit after the transaction cost. Relayers still need to price in capital and risk, so small transfers can face higher fees relative to the amount moved.

Do relayers ever lose money?

Yes. A relayer can lose money when costs or losses exceed the fee it earns. A gas spike can shrink its margin, slow repayment can keep capital tied up, and a source reorganization after an early payout can leave the relayer exposed to an unrepaid fill.
