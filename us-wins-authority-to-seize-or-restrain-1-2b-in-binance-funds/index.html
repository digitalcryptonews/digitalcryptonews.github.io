US wins authority to seize or restrain $1.2B in Binance funds

The order covers part of $2.7 billion frozen with private-sector partners after the October Binance theft, while laundering through bridges, mixers and exchanges continues.

The Finality DeskNov 15, 2026 · 12:57 UTC3 min read#0b38df

U.S. authorities have court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, as investigators and private-sector partners continue tracing funds from an October theft exceeding $6 billion. The Justice Department’s announcement says the authorized amount is part of more than $2.7 billion frozen so far through collective government and industry efforts.

The distinction between frozen assets and seized assets matters. The government says more than $2.7 billion has been frozen; it has obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of that total. The announcement does not say that the entire $1.2 billion has been transferred to government custody or forfeited. The FBI’s October 31 cyber alert attributes the theft to North Korean actors it calls TraderTraitor and identifies cryptocurrency addresses associated with the laundering activity.

The Justice Department says the theft occurred on or about October 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets. Since then, federal agencies have worked with virtual asset service providers to interdict funds. The department says the effort involves its National Security Division and Criminal Division, the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the District of Columbia and law-enforcement partners. It does not disclose a transaction-by-transaction accounting of the frozen or court-authorized assets.

The recovery effort is unfolding across blockchain networks and intermediary services. The FBI says TraderTraitor actors have converted stolen assets primarily into Ether, with further laundering expected through decentralized finance and instant-swap services. The Justice Department says the actors continue to move assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. Those routes complicate tracing by dispersing value across services and networks, while also creating points where service providers may identify and block transactions.

That is why the FBI’s alert asks virtual asset service providers, including bridge and exchange operators, to block transactions with or derived from the listed addresses. The request targets activity associated with known wallet addresses; it is not a network-wide protocol freeze. A public blockchain can record transfers between addresses, but a court order alone does not change consensus rules or prevent a self-custodied wallet from signing a transaction. Interdiction depends on controlling or cooperating with services that can restrict access to assets they hold or process.

The next legal step may be forfeiture. The Associated Press reports that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. That process is separate from freezing assets: a freeze restricts movement, while forfeiture adjudicates ownership and can support returning property to its rightful claimant. The Justice Department says efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the Binance funds are ongoing, so the new authorization marks a legal step in recovery rather than a final accounting.
