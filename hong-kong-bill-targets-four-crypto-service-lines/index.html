Hong Kong bill targets four crypto service lines

A Hong Kong bill would extend SFC licensing to crypto dealing, advice, portfolio management and custody, setting a new compliance perimeter for service firms.

The Finality DeskNov 23, 2026 · 16:25 UTC4 min read#3b590b

Hong Kong has gazetted a bill that would require virtual asset dealing, advisory, management and custody businesses operating in the city to be licensed by or registered with the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC). The government’s announcement on the Virtual Asset Services Legislation (Amendment) Bill 2026 says the proposal would amend the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance (Cap. 615). The bill is scheduled for its first reading in the Legislative Council on Dec. 2.

Gazettal publishes the proposal; it does not make the licensing regime law. The bill must proceed through the Legislative Council, and the government’s announcement does not give a commencement date. Until then, the new requirements remain proposed. That distinction matters for firms planning services in Hong Kong: the announcement establishes the direction of travel, but it does not establish when a provider would need authorization.

Four services enter the proposed perimeter

The government lists four activities: dealing, advisory, management and custody. Each names a different service line in the virtual asset market. Dealing covers firms whose business is handling virtual asset transactions; advisory covers firms that provide advice; management covers firms that manage virtual assets; and custody covers firms that safeguard them. The announcement does not set out detailed definitions or exemptions for these activities, so their precise boundaries cannot be inferred from the category names alone.

For a provider, the proposed trigger is carrying on a business of providing one of those services in Hong Kong. The government says the provider would need an SFC licence or registration and would face a fit-and-proper test and other relevant regulatory requirements. That makes the proposal activity-based: a firm’s service would determine whether it falls within a category, rather than its label as an exchange, broker or technology company.

The distinction could matter where one business performs multiple functions. A firm that both advises clients and manages assets, for example, would be operating across two named categories. The announcement does not specify whether a single licence could cover more than one service, or how overlapping activities would be handled. Those questions depend on the bill’s detailed provisions and subsequent regulatory requirements.

Bank and stored-value operators also face registration

The proposal separately addresses banks and stored-value facility licensees already regulated by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA). If they wish to provide any virtual asset service in Hong Kong, they would also have to register with the SFC. The government says the bill would give both the SFC and HKMA powers to implement the regime under statutory requirements. It does not set out in the announcement how those powers would be divided in day-to-day supervision.

That registration requirement means existing financial-sector status would not, by itself, authorize a bank or stored-value operator to provide virtual asset services under the proposal. The firm would have to enter the SFC process as well. For non-bank providers, the announcement describes the requirement as licensing or registration with the SFC, subject to fitness-and-propriety and other regulatory requirements.

Legislative step comes before any start date

The proposal extends Hong Kong’s existing virtual asset framework beyond trading platforms and stablecoin issuers, according to CoinDesk’s report on the bill. The government says the new regimes would align with standards for conventional financial services and draw on international approaches to anti-money-laundering controls and investor protection.

The next dated step is the Dec. 2 first reading. The government has not announced when the proposed licensing obligations would begin, nor does its announcement specify capital thresholds, application procedures or transitional arrangements. Those details will determine how existing service providers move into the framework if the bill is enacted.
