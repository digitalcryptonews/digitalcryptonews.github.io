Three Routes for Moving BTC Across Chains After RenBridge

BTC can cross chains through threshold-signed tBTC, custodian-issued WBTC, or exchange-wrapped cbBTC; each route trades reach and convenience for a different trust model.

The Finality DeskOct 16, 2026 · 01:55 UTC5 min read#4c0fe0

After RenBridge’s Ren 1.0 route was sunset, BTC holders could still reach smart-contract chains through three different mechanisms: a threshold-operated bridge, a custodian-issued token, or an exchange’s wrapped-BTC service. Each path creates a token claim on another chain, but they place control over the underlying bitcoin in different hands. The key choice is whether you value permissionless minting, a widely traded token, or a direct exchange workflow. Before using the old RenBridge route as a reference point, review these RenBridge custody and redemption questions; they help clarify which risks matter when bitcoin leaves its native chain.

How does tBTC move BTC to another chain?

tBTC moves BTC through a Bitcoin deposit address controlled by a threshold signer group, then issues tBTC on the selected destination chain. A user generates a deposit through the Threshold interface, sends BTC to that address, and the bridge’s operators verify the deposit before the corresponding token is minted. The BTC remains on Bitcoin; tBTC is an ERC-20 or chain-native representation governed by the bridge’s contracts and signer set.

The custody model replaces a single custodian with a randomly selected group of Threshold operators using threshold cryptography. A threshold of signers must agree to act on the Bitcoin wallet. This raises the cost of unilateral seizure, but it does not remove trust: users depend on the signer majority, bridge contracts, and the procedures that connect Bitcoin deposits to token issuance.

To return to Bitcoin, the holder requests redemption and burns tBTC on the destination chain. The bridge records the request, and the signer set releases BTC to the specified Bitcoin address. The route is permissionless and does not require an authorized merchant, but it can take longer than a token swap because Bitcoin confirmation and signer execution are part of the path. Fees, supported destination networks, and minimum deposit requirements can change, so check the live interface before sending.

How does WBTC differ from a threshold bridge?

WBTC is issued against BTC held by a custodian, with authorized merchants handling mint and redemption requests. A merchant initiates a mint, sends BTC to the custodian, and the custodian verifies the deposit before minting WBTC to the merchant on the destination chain. Most users obtain WBTC by buying or swapping it from existing token supply rather than making a direct mint request.

For redemption, a user sends WBTC to an authorized merchant. The merchant arranges the burn, and the custodian releases BTC. The ERC-20 token can then move through Ethereum applications like other tokens, but its backing depends on the custodian’s reserves and the ability of merchants to process redemptions. On-chain supply and reserve records help users inspect issuance; they do not make the underlying BTC self-custodied.

WBTC is useful when the destination application already has liquidity and integrations for it. The trade-off is institutional trust and a merchant-mediated exit. Some versions also exist on other chains, where a bridge or issuer-specific mechanism may govern movement. Verify the exact network and token contract: the same ticker or logo does not prove that a token is an authorized, redeemable WBTC representation.

When does an exchange-wrapped BTC route make sense?

Exchange-wrapped BTC suits users who already hold BTC with an exchange that issues a token on the destination chain. Coinbase’s cbBTC, for example, is backed by BTC held by Coinbase. Eligible customers can send BTC from Coinbase to supported addresses on Base or Ethereum and receive cbBTC at a one-to-one conversion; depositing cbBTC back into a Coinbase account converts it to BTC.

This path removes the need to find a separate Bitcoin deposit address or interact with a bridge contract directly. The exchange controls the underlying BTC and the conversion process, while the user controls the cbBTC token after it reaches a self-custody wallet. Availability depends on supported networks and customer eligibility. It also means that getting native BTC back depends on the exchange accepting the token and completing the conversion.

These routes solve different problems. For a direct, permissionless deposit and redemption, tBTC avoids an authorized merchant but relies on a threshold signer group. For broad DeFi liquidity, WBTC may be easier to use, with custodian and merchant dependencies. For an exchange customer moving BTC into supported networks, cbBTC reduces steps but keeps custody with the issuer.

  • Confirm the destination chain and exact token contract before sending.
  • Check that the route supports redemption to the Bitcoin address format you control.
  • Account for Bitcoin transaction fees, destination-chain gas, bridge or issuer fees, and minimums.
  • Do not treat old renBTC balances or unofficial tokens with similar names as redeemable BTC without verifying their specific exit path.

The practical rule is to choose the exit before the entry. A wrapped token is useful only if its contract, liquidity, and redemption path match the chain and application where you plan to use it. The best route for most users is the one with a verifiable destination token and a clear way back to native BTC.
