OCC leaves stablecoin rewards to case-by-case review

The OCC's GENIUS Act rule keeps direct issuer yield barred but leaves exchange rewards to case-by-case review, with no safe harbor as it takes effect in 2027.

The Finality DeskOct 23, 2026 · 08:48 UTC3 min read#168fc9

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency finalized its GENIUS Act stablecoin rule on Oct. 21, leaving crypto platforms able to offer some interest-like rewards while requiring the agency to assess each arrangement against the law’s ban on issuer yield. The OCC’s final rule bulletin says it takes effect Jan. 18, 2027.

The distinction turns on who pays and why. The GENIUS Act bars a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from paying interest or yield to a holder solely in connection with holding, using or retaining the coin. It does not automatically bar every payment from a separate exchange or custodian. But the OCC’s final rule gives those third-party programs no blanket exemption: the agency will apply the statutory test to the arrangement’s facts.

What changed in the OCC’s final rule?

The OCC removed a proposed rebuttable presumption that could have treated certain issuer relationships with third parties as evidence of prohibited yield. It retained authority to address evasion. Under the final rule, an issuer cannot sidestep the ban by paying an agent to make a payment that the issuer could not make directly. The agency says an intermediary payment can still fall within the prohibition if it is made on the issuer’s behalf and solely because a customer holds, uses or retains the stablecoin.

The rule also rejects a safe harbor for programs that were already running when Congress enacted the law. The OCC says those rewards will be assessed under the same standard as later programs. It has not defined “interest” or “yield” in the rule and says labels do not control: calling a payment a reward does not determine whether it is prohibited.

When can a platform reward a stablecoin holder?

There is no categorical answer in the final rule. The statute’s test asks whether a payment is interest or yield and whether it is made solely in connection with holding, using or retaining a payment stablecoin. A payment made partly for another reason may fall outside that prohibition, but the OCC says transaction-based rewards require a fact-specific assessment. It will consider particular arrangements as they arise.

That leaves platforms without a pre-approved template for rewards funded from stablecoin reserve income or routed through an issuer partner. The CoinDesk report on the OCC’s rule notes the agency did not provide examples of which reward structures would pass. The OCC also declined to create a general safe harbor for white-label partners or payments for services, though it says bona fide service payments generally would not be prohibited absent facts to the contrary.

Who is covered, and what remains unresolved?

The rule covers payment stablecoin issuers under OCC jurisdiction and certain custody activities at OCC-supervised entities. It is the OCC’s part of the GENIUS Act framework, not a final, uniform decision by every federal regulator. The agency says Bank Secrecy Act, anti-money laundering and sanctions requirements will come in separate rulemaking with the Treasury Department. Other agencies are also responsible for parts of the act.

The practical line is narrower than a blanket approval for exchange rewards. Stablecoin issuers remain barred from paying yield solely for holding, using or retaining their coins; third-party programs remain possible only insofar as their structure and purpose do not make them issuer yield or an evasion of that ban. The Associated Press reported that Coinbase, Robinhood and other platforms offer such rewards, while banking groups argue the products could draw deposits away from banks and reduce lending capacity. Those concerns did not produce the broader prohibition banks sought in the OCC’s final rule.
