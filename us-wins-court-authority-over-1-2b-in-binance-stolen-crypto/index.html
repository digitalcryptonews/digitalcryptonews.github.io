US Wins Court Authority Over $1.2B in Binance-Stolen Crypto

US authorities say they froze more than $2.7 billion from the Binance theft and secured court authority over $1.2 billion, while tracing continues.

The Finality DeskNov 16, 2026 · 12:50 UTC3 min read#f531e1

US authorities have obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, while the government and private-sector partners have frozen more than $2.7 billion in total. The distinction matters: the court-authorized amount is part of the larger frozen pool, and the legal process to take ownership of it is still underway.

The Department of Justice said the theft occurred on or about October 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets. The FBI identifies the North Korean military actors behind it as TraderTraitor. In its November 12 announcement, the Justice Department said law enforcement worked with virtual asset service providers, or VASPs, to interdict the stolen assets. The agencies and providers have so far frozen more than $2.7 billion and obtained court authorization covering more than $1.2 billion of that amount.

A freeze and a seizure are different steps. A freeze prevents assets held or controlled by a cooperating service from being moved; the court order supplies legal authority to seize or restrain specified assets. The order does not reverse blockchain transactions or alter a network’s consensus history. The Justice Department’s account describes coordination with VASPs as the means used to stop assets from moving beyond law enforcement’s reach.

The scale of the freeze reflects that intervention across services can interrupt a laundering path before every transfer is complete. But it does not mean all stolen assets are recovered or under government custody. The Justice Department says the effort to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the assets remains active. It also says TraderTraitor actors continue laundering through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. Those routes can move assets between networks, obscure transaction links or introduce intermediaries, forcing investigators to follow funds across services and chains.

The Associated Press reported on the seizure announcement and noted that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered funds. That makes the court authorization a legal milestone, not a final ruling on ownership or a distribution plan. The public announcement does not identify which assets or addresses are covered by the authorization, or how much of the frozen balance can ultimately be forfeited.

For now, the confirmed figures describe different stages of the recovery effort: more than $2.7 billion frozen through combined government and private-sector action, and more than $1.2 billion subject to court-authorized seizure or restraint. The ongoing tracing work will determine whether additional assets can be located and whether they can be brought within reach of investigators and the courts.
