Hong Kong bill extends crypto licensing to four service lines

Hong Kong gazetted a bill to license virtual-asset dealers, advisers, managers and custodians, extending SFC oversight beyond trading platforms and into asset handling.

The Finality DeskNov 22, 2026 · 08:12 UTC3 min read#3e25ad

Hong Kong gazetted a bill on Nov. 20 that would require virtual-asset dealing, advisory, management and custody businesses to obtain a licence from or register with the Securities and Futures Commission (SFC). The Hong Kong government’s announcement says the Virtual Asset Services Legislation (Amendment) Bill 2026 would amend the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing Ordinance. It is a proposal: the government says the bill is scheduled for its first reading in the Legislative Council on Dec. 2.

Which businesses would enter the licensing perimeter?

The bill covers firms carrying on a business in Hong Kong that provides any of four virtual-asset services: dealing, advisory, management or custody. The required route would be licensing by or registration with the SFC. Banks and stored-value facility licensees supervised by the Hong Kong Monetary Authority would also have to register with the SFC to provide virtual-asset services, according to the government announcement.

The scope follows separate regulatory categories rather than treating every activity as exchange operation. In May, the SFC and Financial Services and the Treasury Bureau said the proposed advisory regime would align with Type 4 regulated activity under the Securities and Futures Ordinance, while management would align with Type 9. Their consultation conclusions set those categories against advising on securities and asset management, respectively.

How do dealing and custody differ?

Dealing covers the business of arranging or executing virtual-asset transactions; custody concerns safeguarding client assets and the means to transfer them. The government’s earlier consultation framework included brokerage, block trades and conversions between virtual assets or between virtual assets and fiat money as dealing activity. It defined custody around safekeeping client assets or transfer-enabling instruments, including private keys.

The SFC and bureau’s December 2025 conclusions said the dealing regime would largely align with Type 1 dealing in securities. They described private-key safekeeping as the core custody risk. The four regimes therefore map distinct operational functions onto licensing categories: transaction services, recommendations, portfolio management and control of client assets. The November announcement does not detail the final requirements for each service line.

What changes for firms if the bill passes?

Covered providers would face fit-and-proper tests and other regulatory requirements. The bill would give the SFC and HKMA powers to implement the regime under the statutory framework. The government says the standards are intended to align with those applied to conventional financial services in Hong Kong and to reflect anti-money-laundering and investor-protection standards.

The move follows Hong Kong’s licensing regime for virtual-asset trading platforms and its separate stablecoin issuer regime. Extending coverage to intermediaries, managers and custodians would bring more points in the service chain under formal supervision. For a dealing firm, the regulated activity can occur through a physical outlet or another platform; custody places control over client assets or private keys in scope. That makes the proposed boundary relevant even where a business does not operate a trading platform.

What is confirmed, and what remains open?

The government has gazetted the bill, but it has not yet become law. Dec. 2 is the scheduled first reading, not an implementation date. The Nov. 20 announcement does not set a commencement date or spell out transitional arrangements, exemptions or detailed compliance rules. Those points cannot be treated as settled from the gazetting announcement.

The legislation would complete a broader licensing framework only if enacted and implemented. Until then, the four service categories describe proposed obligations, not a live new licence requirement. Firms assessing the perimeter will need to distinguish the announcement’s confirmed scope from the detailed rules that still depend on the bill’s passage and subsequent implementation.
