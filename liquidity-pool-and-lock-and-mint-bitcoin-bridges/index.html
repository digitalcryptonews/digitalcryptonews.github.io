Liquidity Pool and Lock-and-Mint Bitcoin Bridges

Liquidity pool bridge vs lock and mint bridge for Bitcoin: choose a pool for native coins, or lock and mint for BTC exposure on an EVM chain.

The Finality DeskNov 25, 2026 · 22:17 UTC5 min read#269717

After a transfer, the right Bitcoin bridge design depends on what you want to hold: use a liquidity pool bridge to receive a native asset on the other chain, or a lock-and-mint bridge to hold BTC exposure as a token on an EVM chain. A pool swap pays out an asset that already exists; lock-and-mint creates a token backed by BTC held in reserve, so the output and the ongoing exposure differ.

What do you receive from each Bitcoin bridge?

A liquidity pool bridge swaps native BTC for an asset already available on the destination chain. THORChain and Chainflip use liquidity pools to swap native BTC, so the user ends with the destination asset rather than a newly minted claim on BTC. Once the swap is complete, there is no outstanding bridge claim attached to that output.

A lock-and-mint bridge gives you a token representing BTC held by a custodian or a set of collateralized operators. The token can be used on the destination chain, but its value and redemption depend on the backing BTC and the mechanism that holds it. You continue to carry that bridge exposure for as long as you hold the token.

TeleSwap is a lock-and-mint example: its teleBTC is minted against BTC held by collateralized Lockers. The Lockers and their collateral are part of the backing arrangement, so teleBTC is a claim connected to that system rather than a native coin issued by the destination chain. A comparison of teleswap vs thorchain adds context on how TeleSwap’s model differs from a pool-based route.

How does transfer size affect the choice?

Pool depth sets how much a liquidity pool bridge can swap before price impact becomes significant. A transfer that is small relative to the available depth may have limited impact; as the transfer grows relative to the pool, the exchange rate worsens. The cost driver is the size of the trade compared with the liquidity available for that route.

Lock-and-mint output is based on the BTC amount, at a 1:1 rate minus fees. That makes the output less sensitive to pool depth, which can suit a large transfer when pool liquidity is shallow relative to the amount being moved. The trade-off is that the resulting token remains dependent on its backing and redemption path.

Say you want to move a large amount of BTC and keep BTC exposure on an EVM chain. Lock-and-mint avoids the pool-depth price impact that could reduce a pool swap’s output. If your goal is instead to end with the other chain’s native asset, the pool still matches that goal, but check the available depth against the transfer size.

Which design has more ongoing exposure?

A pool bridge completes a swap into an asset that already exists, so you do not keep a wrapped-BTC claim after the transaction. You still hold the risks of the asset you received and its chain, but the bridge’s BTC backing is not an ongoing dependency of that output.

A minted BTC token keeps the bridge dependency alive while you hold it. The token relies on the BTC held in custody or by Lockers and on your ability to redeem it through the bridge’s process. With teleBTC, that means the BTC held by TeleSwap’s collateralized Lockers backs the token. The bridge risk lasts as long as you hold teleBTC; redeeming it for native BTC is the exit from that claim.

That difference can matter more than the initial transfer. A pool swap leaves you with a destination asset and no outstanding claim on BTC. A lock-and-mint transfer leaves you with BTC exposure in token form, alongside the need to trust the backing and redemption arrangement for as long as the token remains in your wallet.

Choose a liquidity pool bridge when you want a native coin on the other chain and the pool has enough depth for your transfer. Choose a lock-and-mint bridge when you want BTC exposure as a token on an EVM chain, especially for a large transfer relative to pool depth. The first choice ends in a native asset; the second preserves BTC exposure with an ongoing backing dependency.

What do readers ask next?

Can a pool bridge run out of BTC liquidity?

A pool bridge can lack enough available BTC-side liquidity for a requested swap. Pool depth limits how much can move at a given price, and a transfer large relative to that depth faces greater price impact. The relevant check is liquidity on the route for the transfer size.

How is teleBTC redeemed for native BTC?

teleBTC is backed by BTC held by collateralized Lockers, and redemption means exchanging the token through the bridge arrangement for native BTC. The token remains dependent on that backing and redemption path until it is redeemed or otherwise transferred.

Which design has lower fees for small transfers?

There is no universal lower-fee design based on the bridge type alone. Pool swaps are affected by price impact relative to depth; lock-and-mint output is 1:1 minus fees. Compare the costs for the route and amount you plan to move.

Is WBTC lock-and-mint too?

Yes. WBTC is a wrapped Bitcoin token backed by BTC held in custody, so it follows the lock-and-mint model: BTC backs a token that can move on another chain, and holders depend on the backing and redemption arrangement.
