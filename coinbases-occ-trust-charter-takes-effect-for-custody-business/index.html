Coinbase’s OCC trust charter takes effect for custody business

Coinbase’s OCC trust charter took effect Nov. 6, placing its fiduciary digital-asset custody business under federal supervision without authorizing deposit-taking.

The Finality DeskNov 10, 2026 · 14:50 UTC3 min read#dcb7a7

Coinbase’s national trust charter took effect on Nov. 6, placing its planned fiduciary digital-asset custody business under the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency’s federal framework. The company announced final approval on Nov. 9. The OCC’s filing record lists Coinbase National Trust Company as a trust bank and records the charter as effective Nov. 6.

Coinbase says the charter lets it expand custody under federal oversight. It does not convert the exchange into a conventional deposit-taking bank. The OCC’s application record identifies the institution as a national trust bank; Coinbase’s announcement of final approval describes the federal custody framework.

What can Coinbase National Trust Company do under the charter?

The OCC’s April decision describes a trust company whose core activity is digital-asset custody in a fiduciary capacity, primarily for institutional clients. That means the bank holds client assets subject to fiduciary duties; it is not authorized by this charter to take retail deposits or make commercial loans. The institution is a wholly owned subsidiary of Coinbase Global.

The OCC decision also describes related transaction services for custody customers. Customers may accept, hold and transfer fiat currency and digital assets within their custodial accounts, but those services are limited to custody customers and to assets held in custody. Fiat held in custody is kept in “for benefit of” accounts at third-party banks.

For access to certain affiliate products, the trust company may act as a finder. The OCC decision names staking, Prime Trading and Prime Financing as services offered by Coinbase affiliates. The trust company itself will not provide those underlying services. The OCC also said the proposed bank did not plan to issue stablecoins.

What changes with final approval?

The April decision was preliminary and conditional: Coinbase still had to meet pre-opening requirements before receiving authorization to commence business. The OCC’s application record now marks the filing “Consummated/Effective” on Nov. 6. Coinbase’s announcement says the OCC gave final approval the previous Friday.

The charter shifts the trust activities into a federal supervisory framework. Coinbase said the move builds on its New York regulatory base: Coinbase received a New York BitLicense in 2017, and Coinbase Custody Trust Company received a New York limited-purpose trust charter in 2018. The OCC decision said Coinbase planned to migrate its existing custody business from that New York trust company to the national trust bank over a three-year de novo period.

Does the charter authorize ordinary banking?

No. The OCC’s April approval required the bank to limit its operations to trust-company activities and related services. It also said the bank would not be an insured depository institution. The charter therefore does not provide FDIC-insured deposits or grant authority to offer ordinary checking and savings accounts.

The OCC’s conditions also put limits on how the bank can change course. During its first three years, significant deviations from its business plan require prior written notice and a written OCC determination of no objection. The initial approval required at least $60 million in Tier 1 capital, with a specified share held in eligible liquid assets, plus liquid assets equal to 180 days of operating expenses. The OCC said it would supervise compliance with applicable requirements, including Bank Secrecy Act and sanctions controls.

The final charter gives Coinbase a federal route for custody and closely related services. Its operating scope remains tied to the trust-bank model and to OCC supervision.
