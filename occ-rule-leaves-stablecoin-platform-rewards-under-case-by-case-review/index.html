OCC rule leaves stablecoin platform rewards under case-by-case review

The OCC’s GENIUS Act rule bars issuers from paying yield to holders but leaves platform rewards to a facts-and-circumstances review, with no blanket safe harbor.

The Finality DeskOct 24, 2026 · 07:29 UTC3 min read#47a3de

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency’s new GENIUS Act rule keeps stablecoin issuers from paying yield to holders while leaving rewards routed through crypto platforms subject to case-by-case review. The OCC announced the final rule on October 21; it takes effect January 18, 2027. The Associated Press report says the approach allows exchanges such as Coinbase and Robinhood to continue offering interest-like rewards, despite banks’ warnings that the programs could draw deposits away from lenders.

The OCC’s final-rule bulletin implements the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act, or GENIUS Act, for issuers and related activities under the agency’s jurisdiction. The distinction turns on who pays the holder and why: the statute bars a permitted payment stablecoin issuer from paying interest or yield to a holder solely in connection with holding, using or retaining the stablecoin. It does not categorically ban every reward paid by another company.

What changed in the OCC’s yield rule?

The OCC dropped a proposed rebuttable presumption that would have treated certain issuer arrangements with affiliates or related third parties as violations of the yield prohibition unless the issuer rebutted that presumption. In the final rule, the agency says it will instead apply the statute to the facts and circumstances of each arrangement. It declined to publish a list of permitted or prohibited reward structures.

That change leaves room for platforms to distribute rewards tied to stablecoins, as AP reported, but it does not create a blanket regulatory safe harbor. The OCC says it will review issuer arrangements through applications and ongoing supervision. It also retains authority to act against transactions that violate or evade the prohibition. The legal question remains whether an issuer is paying yield to holders, directly or through an arrangement that amounts to evasion.

When can a platform reward become an issuer violation?

The final rule gives no automatic answer based on the label “exchange reward” or on whether a program predates the GENIUS Act. The OCC says older programs will be evaluated under the same standard as newer ones. It also rejected a broad safe harbor for white-label arrangements, where an issuer creates a stablecoin on a partner’s behalf or under the partner’s branding. In those cases, the relationship and the payment’s connection to holding, using or retaining the coin matter.

For platforms, that means the structure and funding of a program matter more than its marketing name. AP described exchanges passing along earnings from reserve assets backing stablecoins. But the OCC’s rule does not declare that every such arrangement is permissible. Issuers remain subject to the statutory yield restriction, and the agency will assess the specific contracts and relationships involved.

Why are banks focused on the rewards?

Stablecoin reserves can include bank deposits and U.S. Treasury securities, which earn interest. Crypto companies have sought to share some of that return with customers. The banking industry argues that higher yields on stablecoin balances could move deposits out of banks and reduce funds available for lending. AP reported that the American Bankers Association said the OCC’s decision would threaten funds banks use to make loans. Those are industry concerns, not findings in the OCC rule.

The final rule establishes the OCC’s framework for issuers within its jurisdiction, including requirements for reserves, redemption, risk management and supervision. Its treatment of rewards leaves a consequential boundary for future enforcement: the issuer cannot pay yield to holders solely for holding, using or retaining a payment stablecoin, while third-party reward arrangements turn on their facts and whether they evade that prohibition.
