What Does a Good Rugcheck Result Look Like? A Clean Report

A good rugcheck result shows revoked mint and freeze authority, mostly burned or locked liquidity, dispersed holders, and no danger-level risk items in the report.

The Finality DeskNov 27, 2026 · 05:40 UTC4 min read#37db82

A good rugcheck result shows Mint authority and Freeze authority set to none, most Liquidity pool tokens burned or locked, no non-pool wallet with a large share, and no danger-level risk items. Each result matters because it removes a direct route to dilute holders, freeze accounts or withdraw liquidity. A rugcheck, or token scam risk check, helps you see how those controls affect your decision to interact with a token.

What the report checks and shows

A token risk check reads on-chain details and turns them into risk findings you can inspect. It looks at who can change token supply or account access, how liquidity is held, and how token holdings are distributed. The report gives you both an overall assessment and individual risk lines; the lines show which conditions produced that assessment.

Mint authority controls whether more units of a token can be created. When it is revoked, no new supply can ever be minted. Freeze authority controls whether token accounts can be frozen. With that authority revoked, the holder of the authority has no such control. These are separate permissions, so a clean result has both set to none.

Liquidity pool tokens represent a claim on a pool’s liquidity. Burned LP tokens sit in an account nobody controls, so that liquidity cannot be withdrawn through those tokens. Locked LP tokens are subject to a lock rather than being burned; look at the lock details and duration shown in the report before treating them as unavailable. A locked portion can become accessible later.

Holder concentration shows whether supply sits across many wallets or is dominated by a few. Exclude pool and other non-holder addresses when interpreting the largest holder figure, then look for a single non-pool wallet with a large share. The Token Program field also matters: a standard Token Program mint is the clean example here. A Token-2022 mint calls for attention to any listed features or risk lines rather than an assumption that the program label alone decides the result.

A clean report you can picture

Take a token report with Mint authority set to none, Freeze authority set to none, 100% of LP tokens burned, and a largest non-pool holder below a few percent. The mint uses the standard Token Program, and the risk list has no danger-level item. That is a strong-looking result because the report shows no active authority to expand supply or freeze accounts, and the burned LP tokens are beyond anyone’s control.

Those values are a practical example, not a universal pass threshold. “A few percent” is a quick way to recall what a dispersed holder profile can look like; it is not a cutoff that makes every token safe. A wallet’s share matters alongside liquidity depth, the other large holders and any warnings in the report. Several wallets with similar large balances can still leave supply concentrated among a small group.

Read the risk lines, not just the label

An overall “Good” can still come with a warn-level item, such as mutable metadata. That warning matters more than the aggregate if it changes what you are comfortable accepting. Read the line, identify the specific condition it describes, and decide whether that condition creates a risk for your intended use. The summary label compresses findings; it does not make each finding irrelevant.

Low liquidity is another warning to weigh separately. A token can have revoked authorities and burned LP tokens while the pool remains small. Those controls reduce particular ways to alter supply or withdraw liquidity, but they do not make a thin pool deep. Consider whether the available liquidity fits the size of the transaction you have in mind, and treat a low-liquidity warning as its own constraint.

Mutable metadata is not the same risk as an active mint authority: one concerns whether token information can change, while the other concerns whether supply can increase. Keep the findings distinct. A token scam risk check is useful when it helps you connect each warning to the control or distribution detail behind it, rather than letting one “Good” label settle the decision.

When to check again

Rescan right before buying or interacting with the token. Authorities can change, and LP status can change through later on-chain writes, so an earlier report may no longer describe the current state. Treat the result as a time-specific reading of the token’s conditions. If you return to a token after a delay, run the check again and compare the authority, liquidity and holder findings before acting.

For a fast review, confirm both authorities are none, inspect whether LP tokens are burned or locked, look at the largest non-pool holders, and scan every risk line for warnings. That gives you a report you can judge in seconds while keeping the specific risks visible.
