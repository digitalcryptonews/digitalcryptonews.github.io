Delegated Staking Gives Validators Weight, Not Token Control

Delegated staking in crypto lets you assign tokens’ validation weight to a validator, earn rewards after commission, and share the risk of slashing.

The Finality DeskNov 20, 2026 · 05:16 UTC4 min read#c93365

Delegated staking lets you assign your tokens’ staking weight to a validator while you keep ownership, provided you accept that the stake can be penalized. The validator runs the node and earns rewards for helping secure the network; it takes a commission, and you receive the remainder. Delegation exists because running a reliable node takes hardware and skills many token holders lack.

Delegation assigns validation weight while you keep ownership

In delegated staking, you choose a validator and commit tokens through the chain’s staking process. The tokens count toward the validator’s stake, which helps determine its role in consensus. The validator operates the node; you do not need to maintain its hardware or software.

Delegation hands over validation weight, not spending power. On chains that support this arrangement, the validator cannot transfer or spend your delegated tokens. The protocol still treats them as stake, though, so penalties can reach them. Ownership and risk are separate: you retain the claim to the tokens, but you share consequences tied to the validator’s conduct.

Rewards depend on the chain’s rules and the validator’s performance. The validator may take a commission from rewards before the rest is credited to you. Poor uptime can mean missed rewards or penalties, while protocol-defined misconduct can trigger slashing: destruction of some staked tokens. The amount at risk depends on the network and the event.

Take two delegators with the same amount of ATOM and the same validator. If the validator earns rewards and avoids a slash, both receive their share after commission. If that validator is slashed, both delegators’ bonded stake can be reduced. The decisive condition is the validator’s record under the chain’s rules, not whether a delegator personally ran the node.

On Cosmos Hub, you can delegate ATOM to a validator. To withdraw it, you undelegate; that starts a 21-day unbonding period before the tokens become transferable. During that wait, the stake remains exposed to applicable penalties. Cosmos Hub staking parameters document the unbonding rule.

Solana also lets holders delegate stake, but its exit timing follows epochs rather than a fixed 21-day period. Deactivation takes effect progressively at epoch boundaries and can take several epochs, depending on network conditions. So, for a holder with the same goal—freeing delegated tokens—the chain changes the wait: Cosmos Hub has a 21-day unbonding period, while Solana deactivates stake across epoch boundaries. Solana’s staking documentation describes that process.

Ethereum delegation adds an operator layer through eigen layer

Ethereum’s protocol does not natively let a token holder delegate stake to a validator. Operating a validator directly requires at least 32 ETH and node infrastructure. Services can operate a validator for a staker, but that is an arrangement layered on top of Ethereum rather than protocol-level delegation.

Restaking introduces a separate delegation path. eigen layer lets eligible stakers reuse staked ETH or liquid staking tokens to help secure additional services, called actively validated services (AVSs). A restaker can delegate this restaked position to an operator, who runs the AVS work. That can add rewards, along with added slashing exposure if the operator fails its obligations.

The feature that makes this a concrete example is operator delegation: restaking stETH on eigen layer shows how a restaker can assign a restaked asset to an operator for AVS work. The underlying Ethereum stake and the extra service obligations are distinct layers, so assess both before delegating.

Is delegated staking safe?

Delegated staking carries protocol risk and validator or operator risk. A reliable operator can still face technical faults, and a poor choice can expose stake to penalties or slashing. Review commission, performance, and the chain’s unbonding or deactivation rules before committing. Most importantly, confirm what the delegation mechanism permits: a non-custodial delegation preserves your control of token transfers, but it does not make the stake immune to loss.

Delegated staking vs a staking pool: what's the difference?

Delegation assigns your stake to a specific validator or operator under a chain or protocol’s delegation rules. A staking pool combines contributions from multiple users and manages validators for the pool; it may issue a token representing your pool share. With delegation, you track the validator or operator tied to your stake. With a pool, you also rely on the pool’s contracts and accounting for deposits, rewards, and withdrawals.
