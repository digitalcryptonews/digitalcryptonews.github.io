US court authorizes seizure of $1.2B in stolen Binance crypto

The Justice Department won court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in stolen Binance assets, part of a wider effort to freeze the funds.

The Finality DeskNov 15, 2026 · 15:07 UTC3 min read#92f773

The Justice Department has obtained court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step in a broader effort to stop the funds moving through crypto services. The Justice Department’s November 12 announcement says that amount is part of more than $2.7 billion frozen through U.S. government and private-sector action.

The FBI attributes the theft to North Korean military members it calls TraderTraitor. The Justice Department says the theft occurred on or about October 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets. The FBI identified the activity as North Korean cyber activity in an October 31 alert. The $1.2 billion figure therefore describes assets covered by court authority, not the full theft or the total frozen so far.

The distinction matters because the announcement describes several different stages. Service providers and law enforcement partners froze assets to impede their movement. The court authorization permits the government to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of that frozen total. The department has not said that this amount has been returned to Binance or to customers. The effort to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit assets remains ongoing.

The operational response depends on tracing activity across crypto services and getting intermediaries to act. The FBI’s alert on the Binance attack says investigators observed TraderTraitor converting stolen assets primarily into Ether. The bureau expected further laundering through decentralized finance and instant-swap services. It urged virtual-asset providers, including node operators and DeFi services, to block transactions involving listed addresses or funds derived from them.

The Justice Department says TraderTraitor actors continue to move assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. Each route creates a different point for tracing or intervention. A bridge can move value between chains; an exchange or other service may be able to restrict activity involving accounts or addresses it controls. The DOJ announcement does not detail the individual assets, chains or service providers covered by the $1.2 billion court authorization.

That limitation also means the order does not amount to a protocol-level rollback. The FBI is asking intermediaries to block transactions connected to identified addresses, while the Justice Department is pursuing seizure or restraint through legal process. Neither statement says that the underlying blockchain transfers can be reversed by the government. The practical reach of the response depends on tracing the assets and on services acting before funds move beyond their control.

The case now has two parallel measures: an existing freeze exceeding $2.7 billion and court authority covering more than $1.2 billion within it. U.S. officials and private-sector partners continue to trace the remaining stolen assets as investigators seek to disrupt further laundering. The figures describe assets intercepted or subject to legal action so far; they do not establish how much will ultimately be forfeited or recovered.
